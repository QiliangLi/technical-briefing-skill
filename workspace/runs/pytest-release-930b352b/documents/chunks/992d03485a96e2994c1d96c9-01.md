a telemetry-calibrated energy-and-carbon methodology, and a year-long marginal-emissions

Abstract
Large-language-model (LLM) inference is now a material and fast-growing electricity load,
and the marginal carbon intensity of that load varies by more than an order of magnitude across
grid regions and across the day. Routing inference requests toward cleaner regions is therefore
an attractive, low-friction lever — no model retraining, no hardware change, only a placement
decision. We report a live validation of carbon-aware inference routing on real multi-region GPU
testbeds, driven by WattTime marginal operating emissions rate (MOER) signals, with three
properties uncommon in prior work: (1) a realisticblindbaseline that is the actual production
pressure-based router rather than uniform placement; (2) per-request energy attributed from
GPU telemetry (NVIDIA DCGM) via measured concurrency curves rather than nameplate TDP;
and (3) carbon settlement of every request against WattTime'shistoricalMOER, not only the
forecast that drove the routing decision.
The central result is one of feasibility: a MOER signal can steer live inference workloads
across a multi-region GPU testbed, with no observed dispatch failures, as a strict and reversible
overlay on the existing production router. Over multi-day runs on live two-region testbeds,
the signal measurably moved real inference load between regions and settled emissions in the
intended direction — establishing that the mechanism works end-to-end on production hardware.
What a short, two-site live runcannotestablish is the carbon magnitude — how much this saves
across grids and seasons — which we quantify with a year-long historical-MOER replay across
a grid-diverse CONUS fleet (§6). The replay indicates a substantial potential reduction, from
roughly 23% under a soft multiplier policy matching the live production overlay's form up to
34–51% under an aggressive cleanest-first dispatch and alternative treatments of the zero-MOER
curtailment intervals. In the primary replay configuration, carbon-aware placement reduces
modeled GPU-attributable operational emissions by a central 50.9% versus round-robin (51.0%
versus a modeled carbon-blind load balancer) (95% block-bootstrap CI 48.5–53.3%,conditional
on the modeled fleet, routing policy, workload and zero-MOER-credit assumptions). Because
the replay dispatches directly against historical MOER rather than a forecast, these reductions
represent an upper-bound opportunity under the modeled configuration; forecast error would
reduce operationally realized savings. In the freely-placeable decomposition before session pinning,
hourly routing that seeks the lowest MOER contributes approximately 22.4 percentage points —
roughly 40% of the 54.0% placement reduction — beyond the static annual-mean-MOER policy.
These are modeled results for one specified fleet and historical year, not a universal estimate;
§6 states the configuration and its assumptions. We also record a practical observation: when
comparingregions, rank by absolute MOER rather than the percentilesignal-index, which is
normalized within each region and answers atemporal, not a spatial, question. With potential

1 Introduction
The electricity demand of AI inference has moved from a rounding error to a planning constraint for
cloud operators [7, 12], a trajectory flagged early in the ML community's accounting of deep-learning
energy cost [17]. Unlike training, inference iscontinuous, latency-sensitive, and geographically
flexible: the same request can often be served from any of several regions with spare capacity.
Because the marginal carbon intensity of electricity — the emissions of the generator that responds
to one more MWh of demand [1] — differs sharply across grid regions and hours,whereandwhen
an inference request runs materially changes its carbon impact.
Carbon-aware computing has an established literature for batch and deferrable workloads: temporal
shifting of flexible jobs [5], elastic scaling to track clean hours [9], datacenter-level carbon planning
[4, 6], and virtualized energy interfaces for applications [8]. A growing body of work targets ML
specifically [13, 14, 15], and geographical load balancing has long been studied for cost and, more
recently, for carbon and equity [10, 16]. Our contribution isnota new scheduling algorithm. It
is a live systems validation of the simplest possible lever — a per-request routing bias — under
production-like conditions, and a practical observation aboutwhich carbon signalthat lever should
consume.
This distinction matters because the carbon signal a system routes on is a design choice with
non-obvious consequences. WattTime exposes both an absolute MOER (lbs CO2/MWh) and
a normalized signal-index (0–100 percentile of that region's MOER against that region's own
distribution; the exact window is API-version-dependent, see §2.2). The signal-index is an excellent
instrument for thetemporalquestion — "where does this region’s current MOER fall within its
forecast distribution over the next 24 hours?" — which is exactly what a single-site, load-shifting
controller needs. For short inference requests the current interval is the relevant one; a job spanning
more than a single five-minute interval should consult the forecast rather than the instantaneous
value. For thecross-regionquestion — "which of these regions is cleaner right now?" — the absolute
MOER is the more natural basis, since the percentile is defined relative to each region's own temporal
variation.

2 Background and related work
2.1 Marginal vs. average emissions
The carbon impact of achangein electricity consumption is governed by the marginal emissions rate
— the carbon intensity of the marginal generator — not the grid-average rate [1, 19]. WattTime's
MOER operationalizes this as a real-time and forecast signal per grid region (typically the balancing
authority); complementary flow-tracing methods underpin real-time carbon-intensity accounting
for other grids and public datasets [11] (for regions with interchange, MOER itself incorporates
the marginal fuel mix of imports alongside the domestic marginal mix). For a decision thatmoves
load between regions or hours, marginal factors are the correct basis; average factors systematically
misprice the effect of the decision [1, 19]. According to the Greenhouse Gas Protocol, the impact
from interventions that cause a change in electricity use should be accounted for using consequential
methods and marginal emissions rates [28, 29]. All carbon accounting in this work uses MOER.
2.2 The signal-index is a temporal, relative instrument
WattTime additionally publishes a signal-index: a 0–100 value representing thepercentileof a
region's current MOER within that region's own distribution (0 = cleanest for that region, 100 =
dirtiest for that region). The exact normalization window is API-version-dependent — legacy V2
(no longer available) compared against roughly the trailing month, whereas V3 compares against the
region's upcoming 24-hour forecast. These experiments use the WattTime V3 API [26] (co2_moer
from /v3/forecast for live dispatch and/v3/historical for settlement), North-America MOER
model version 2026-03-01 [27] (which updated curtailment handling; §6). This normalization is
deliberatelyrelative to each region, which makes it ideal for a controller asking "should I run my
flexible job now, or wait?" — the question at the heart of temporal-shifting systems [5, 9]. The
relativity that makes it excellent forwithin-region temporaldecisions is precisely why the absolute
MOER is the better basis forcross-region spatialdecisions, as we discuss in §5.3.
2.3 Carbon-aware and energy-aware ML serving
Prior ML-serving work has exploited model-variant selection and spatiotemporal scheduling to
cut inference carbon [13, 14] and has characterized inference-cluster energy and its optimization
[15, 16]. Measurement studies have quantified the carbon intensity of AI in cloud instances and its
regional/temporal variability [18], established methodologies for ML carbon accounting that account
for location and time-of-day effects [20], and characterized the broader cost of AI deployment [12].
Closest to this work, a recent line designs carbon-aware inference serving with operational and
embodied accounting from production traces [23], evaluates geographic LLM serving and scaling on
millions of production requests [24], and performs per-request carbon routing under p95-latency
and accuracy constraints [25]. Our contribution is distinct along four axes those studies do not
jointly cover: a live physical multi-region deployment, marginal-emissions routing, a production
pressure-router control arm, and forecast-to-historical settlement. We otherwise hold the model

3 System design
3.1 Routing term
The production routing fabric (Solyx AI Grid [22]) routes each request by a pressure weight per cell
(a function of live queue depth, load, and capacity). Carbon-awareness is addedmultiplicatively and
default-off: each cell's weight is scaled by a cleanliness multiplier
multiplier(cell) = 1 - w * intensity(region(cell))
where w∈[0, 1]is the carbon weight (0 = off, i.e. the unmodified production router) andintensity
∈[0, 1]is the region's normalized carbon intensity. Withw = 0 the multiplier is identically 1, so
the carbon term is a strict, reversible overlay on production behavior. Region identity is resolved
from a verified datacenter→grid region registry, not from the cloud provider's (frequently incorrect)
region labels.
The current implementation also sets the multiplier to 1 when a region's intensity is unknown or
stale — a neutral default that, we note, can bias traffic toward a missing-data region over one with
a known non-zero penalty. The safer behavior, which we recommend and are adopting, is to disable
the overlay for that routing decision and revert all candidates to the pressure-only baseline rather
than treat unknown as clean.
Thecritical design variableis how intensity is derived from the WattTime signal (§5.3): from
theabsoluteMOER or from thepercentilesignal-index.
3.2 Energy from telemetry, not nameplate
Per-request energy is attributed from a Phase-0 energy characterization: an offline concurrency sweep
on each GPU SKU that measures GPU power via NVIDIA DCGM and fits a joules-per-output-token
curve across load levels. The measured curves are steep — energy per output token falls∼30×from

3.3 Dispatch vs. settled carbon
Routing decisions are necessarily made against the forecast MOER available at decision time. But
the honest carbon attribution must use the historical MOER for the interval in which the request
actually ran. The fabric records the dispatch (forecast) MOER on every request and, after the fact,
settles each request against WattTime's published historical MOER (5-minute resolution) [2] before
any carbon figure is quoted. Historical MOER is itself a modeled estimate that WattTime may later
revise — not a directly metered quantity — so we treat it as the best available post-hoc reference
rather than ground truth. All headline numbers in §5 aresettled.
4 Experimental methodology
Workload:A synthetic but sustained open-loop stream of Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct completions via
vLLM, identical across arms, driven at a 5 arrivals/s session-initiation rate with bounded max-inflight
(256). Single-turn classes emit one request per arrival while multi-turn sessions expand to several
turns each, so the 48 h run produces≈1.96M individual request-turns (≈864k arrivals×≈2.3 turns
per arrival), partitioned across the five arms. Arrival-level class fractions are the workload-generator
defaults — 40% single-turn completions, 50% multi-turn sessions, 10% long-context RAG — which,
at≈3.5 turns per multi-turn session, correspond to≈18% /≈78% /≈4% of request-turns. Single-
turn prompts are lognormal (median≈245 tokens) with 64–512-token decodes; multi-turn prompts
start at≈181 tokens and grow≈200 tokens per turn (2–16 turns, mean≈3.5) with 64–320-token
decodes; and the long-context class carries 2,000–4,000-token prefills with 128–512-token decodes —
the prefill-heavy tail that dominates TTFT under saturation.
Arms:Five routing arms run concurrently over the same workload and the same live MOER stream:
round-robin, off(theproductionpressurerouter—theblindcontrol), and eco-low/eco-med/eco-high
(carbon weights 0.25/0.50/1.00). The comparison we report is eco-high vs off.
Armassignmentandisolation:Eachrequestisassignedtoasinglearmbyaseededrandomization
stratified by traffic class — requests arenotcloned across arms — so the∼1.96M request-turn
count is the total across arms (≈0.39M/arm), and the class mix (and thus the prompt/decode-token
distribution) is balanced across arms in expectation rather than matched per request. The arms
are not isolated replicas: they share the same physical GPUs, queues, and live pressure state.
Consolidation by a carbon arm therefore changes the queue depth and contention the other arms
experience — a treatment-interference (SUTVA) effect we disclose rather than assume away —
so the live latency result isobservational, not a controlled per-arm measurement. Emissions are
compared as per-request attributed gCO2 (attributed energy×settled MOER) aggregated per arm,
with shared/idle GPU power split by work share. Because the live carbon delta is small, we treat
the traffic-placement result as the robust live finding and the settled-carbon delta as directional
only. The off and eco-high arms use the same underlying pressure router and differ in their
assigned carbon multiplier, so their contrast reflects enabling the carbon-aware policy within this
shared-resource environment. Because the arms influence one another's queue and contention state,
the contrast is not an isolated per-arm causal effect.
Reading the live runs:These two-site runs are afeasibility and systemscheck, not a powered
measurement: at only a couple of diurnal cycles per testbed they cannot support confidence intervals.
We therefore report what was observed — whether the signal moved load, the observed latency and
dispatch-failure behavior, and whether attributed emissions settled in the intended direction — and

5 Results
5.1 Systems viability
The fabric ran unattended on the live testbeds for multi-day spans, sustaining approximately 1.96
million request-turns with zero dispatch errors, live 5-minute MOER polling, atomic checkpointing,
and post-hoc settlement. The carbon overlay is a strict multiplicative layer on the production
router and is fully reversible (w=0). This establishes the primary systems claim: carbon-aware
routing is deployable on a production inference fabric with no observed dispatch failures over
the test window, and with a clean off-switch. Reliability dimensions beyond dispatch success —
request-completion/timeout rates, SLO attainment, failover — were not separately measured and
are not claimed here.
5.2 The mechanism moves real load (A100·AZPS + PJM-DC)
Over∼48 hours (∼1.96M logged request-turns total across arms —≈864k session/single-turn
arrivals at 5/s, expanded by multi-turn sessions — and 0 dispatch errors), the eco-high arm settled
1.45% lower attributed GPU operational emissions than the off (blind pressure-router) arm. On
latency: under the deliberately saturating workload, the observed p95 was 18.7 s for the off arm
and 20.9 s for eco-high, an 11.7% increase (the high absolute p95 reflects the deliberately saturating
open-loop load and the 2,000–4,000-token prefills in the long-context class; see §4). Because the
routing arms shared physical GPUs, queues, and pressure state, this is an observational within-run
comparison rather than an isolated causal estimate; the experiment was not designed or powered to
establish a generalizable latency effect or formal SLO non-inferiority. We report the emissions figure
as an observed, non-generalizable descriptive result, not an estimate of expected savings: a single
∼48-hour window, one grid pair, roughly two diurnal cycles, and highly autocorrelated MOER

which supplies the confidence intervals and seasonal structure a single live run cannot.
5.3 An interpretation note: rank regions by absolute MOER (H100 ·ERCOT-
NC + SOCO)
We initially derivedintensity from WattTime's signal-index (percentile). On the H100 testbed the
carbon arm moved load but tracked each region'spercentilerather than itsabsoluteemissions — so
it did not reliably prefer the cleaner region. The reason is a straightforward property of the signal:
The signal-index is a percentile relative to each region's own distribution. When two
regions have very different MOER distributions, equal percentiles do not imply equal
absolute emissions — and the percentile ranking can invert the absolute ranking.
Concretely, Dallas (ERCOT-NC) is a wide-swing grid (MOER historically ranging ∼0–1389
lbs/MWh); Atlanta (SOCO) is narrow (∼1135–1390). A Dallas reading of∼1253 lbs/MWh
sits at ahighpercentile for Dallas, while an Atlanta reading of∼1330 lbs/MWh sits at a compara-
tivelylowerpercentile for Atlanta — even though Dallas is absolutely cleaner (1253 < 1330). A
percentile-driven router therefore sends load to the absolutely-dirtierregion. The signal-index is
doing exactly what it was designed to do (flag each region against its own distribution); it is simply
the wrong metric for a spatial decision.
Fix:Deriving intensity from absolute MOER, normalized by a fixed reference scale, aligns routing
with the absolute ranking: the carbon arm prefers the absolutely-cleaner region — modestly when
regions are close, strongly when one region curtails. We verified the corrected direction in live
telemetry (the carbon arm's share of the cleaner region rose above the blind arm's, tracking the
sign of the absolute MOER gap). It is a one-line change inhow the signal is interpreted— absolute
MOER for spatial comparisons, the percentile for within-region timing. The absolute MOER
(historical and forecast) also serves the within-region timing question, and is what licensed users
typically route on; the signal-index is freely available as a shifting signal but does not expose the
absolute values needed to quantify a carbon reduction.
A note on the signal-index:The signal-index is built for the temporal question and excels at
it; the observation here is simply that cross-regioncomparisonsare better served by the absolute
MOER. As multi-region inference grows, that is a useful and easily-documented clarification for
signal-index consumers (§8).

5.4 Energy characterization
The Phase-0 curves (§3.2) show energy-per-output-token falling∼30×from low to saturating
concurrency on both H100 and A100. Two implications: (i) carbon savings fromroutingcompound
with, and can be dominated by,utilization— an underloaded clean region can emit more per
token than a saturated dirtier one; and (ii) any credible inference-carbon claim must measure
energy at the operating concurrency, not from nameplate TDP, consistent with calls for systematic
energy-and-carbon reporting in ML [21].
5.5 Magnitude is grid- and season-dependent
Live forecasts during the H100 window explained the muted signal: in mid-July, Dallas (ERCOT-NC)
deep curtailment did not appear in the forecast; its forecast MOER stayed pinned in a narrow
∼1187–1279 lbs/MWh band rather than its full∼0–1389 range — under high air-conditioning
demand and weak wind. The usable cross-region spread (peaking∼+180 lbs/MWh) came largely
fromSOCO risinginto its afternoon peak, not from Dallas dipping, and even inverted overnight.
The single most curtailment-rich scenario (CAISO) was unavailable on H100-class hardware for the
entire window. Takeaway: themechanism(absolute-MOER routing) is validated and directionally
correct; themagnitudeis a strong function of grid pair, curtailment depth, and season — a breadth
question best answered by historical-MOER replay across grids and seasons (§6), not by any single
two-site live run.
